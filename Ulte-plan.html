Nature of Light & The 5 Historical Theories

PDF Pages 1–2 • Empedocles, Luminous Classification, and Wave-Particle Duality

Light is a foundational branch of physics and a vital source of radiant energy that stimulates the human retina to produce the sensation of sight. The earliest philosophical concept of light was proposed by Empedocles.

Heuristic Model: Rectilinear Photon Packet Transport
Self-Luminous Bodies

Objects that generate and radiate their own intrinsic light energy.

  • (a) Natural Self-Luminous: The Sun, distant stars.
  • (b) Artificial Luminous: Man-made sources such as burning candles, flames, electric bulbs.
Non-Self-Luminous Bodies

Objects that do not produce their own light; they become visible by reflecting light from luminous bodies.

  • Examples: The Moon, planet Earth, mirrors, furniture.
  • 3 Study Branches: Photometry, Optics (Ray & Wave), and Colours (Chromatography & Spectroscopy).
1. Corpuscular Theory

Sir Isaac Newton

Light consists of tiny elastic particles called corpuscles traveling in straight lines.

2. Wave Theory

Christiaan Huygens

Light propagates in the form of continuous waves; explains reflection and refraction.

3. Electromagnetic Theory

James Clerk Maxwell

Light is electromagnetic radiation capable of traveling through vacuum at 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

4. Quantum Theory

Max Planck & Albert Einstein

Light consists of discrete energy packets called photons, each carrying a definite quantum of energy.

5. Dual-Nature Theory & Material Transmission

Light exhibits both wave and particle properties. When passing through materials, media are classified as: Transparent (clear view: glass, water, air), Translucent (blurred view: frosted glass, butter paper), and Opaque (blocks light: wood, metal, cardboard).

Optically Denser Medium (Water / Glass)

• Light travels slower (lower speed of light).

• Higher refractive index & higher optical density.

• Rarer → Denser: Ray bends TOWARD the normal.

Optically Rarer Medium (Air / Vacuum)

• Light travels faster (higher speed of light).

• Lower refractive index & lower optical density.

• Denser → Rarer: Ray bends AWAY from the normal.

Laws of Reflection, Glancing Angle & Image Types

PDF Pages 3–5 • Specular vs. Diffuse Reflection & Real vs. Virtual Images

The Two Laws of Reflection

1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection: ∠i = ∠r.

2. The incident ray, reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

Glancing Angle Formula

The angle between the incident ray and the reflecting surface itself: g = 90° - i.

Regular vs. Irregular Reflection

Regular: Smooth polished surface (plane mirror); parallel rays stay parallel and form a clear image. Irregular: Rough surface (wallpaper, wood); no clear image is formed.

Real vs. Virtual Image

Real: Rays actually meet; can be caught on a screen; usually inverted (projector, concave mirror). Virtual: Rays appear to meet backward; cannot be obtained on a screen (plane & convex mirror).

Inverted vs. Erect Image

Inverted: Upside down relative to object (associated with real images). Erect: Upright in same orientation as object (associated with virtual images).

Plane Mirror Properties

Forms a virtual, erect image of the same size as the object, laterally inverted, at an equal distance behind the mirror.

f = R / 2  |  R = 2f

Concave: f is -ve • Convex: f is +ve

Mirror Formula
1/f = 1/u + 1/v

Equivalent to 1/v = 1/f - 1/u

Solar Cooker Construction & Archimedes' Story

PDF Pages 9–10 • Thermal Convergence of Parallel Sunlight at Focus F

Solar Cooker Principle: A large concave reflector (constructed from triangular mirror sheets around a dish frame or a TV dish reflector) is directed toward the Sun. Parallel rays of sunlight reflect and converge at the focal point (F), producing high temperatures to heat a cooking vessel placed at F.

Archimedes' Story: Famous historical account in which Archimedes used concave mirrors to concentrate parallel rays of sunlight onto enemy ships to generate intense heat.
